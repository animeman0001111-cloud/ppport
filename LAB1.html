Problem Statement

Coffee cools,
ice cream warms

A cup of coffee at 95°C placed in a room at 20°C — model the temperature change over time using a first-order differential equation.

Temperature change in objects follows an exponential pattern governed by Newton's Law of Cooling. The rate of change is proportional to the difference between the object's temperature and its surroundings.

Newton's Law of Cooling
dT/dt = −k(T − Tₛ)
T= object temperature
Tₛ= surrounding temperature
k= cooling/warming constant
t= time

This is a first-order linear differential equation. Both cooling (coffee from 95°C to 75°C) and warming (ice cream from −15°C to 20°C) are governed by the same equation, confirming its universality.

Analytical Solution

Solving the Equation

Deriving T(t) from the governing differential equation

The solution to Newton's Law of Cooling is found by separating variables and integrating. The result is a decaying exponential that approaches the ambient temperature asymptotically.

  1. The rate of temperature change depends on the difference between object and surrounding temperature
  2. Initially the rate of change is high, then decreases as temperatures equalize
  3. The object never crosses the surrounding temperature
  4. Apply initial condition T(0) = T₀ to find the constant of integration
  5. Compare coffee cooling and ice cream warming using the same k value
Solution Steps
Governing Equation
dT/dt = −k(T − Tₛ)
Separate Variables & Integrate
∫ dT/(T − Tₛ) = −k∫dt
ln|T − Tₛ| = −kt + C
General Solution
T(t) = Tₛ + (T₀ − Tₛ)e^(−kt)
Coffee Cooling · Parameters
T₀ = 95°C, Tₛ = 20°C, k = 0.0715
Ice Cream Warming
T₀ = −15°C, Tₛ = 20°C, k = 0.0715

Conclusion

One Equation, Two Phenomena

Newton's Law of Cooling elegantly models both heat loss and heat gain using a single first-order ODE. The RK4 numerical method provided highly accurate results, confirming that the rate of temperature change is always proportional to the temperature difference — rapidly at first, slowing asymptotically to equilibrium. This experiment establishes the foundation of applied differential equations in thermodynamic systems.
