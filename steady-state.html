Chapter Overview

How systems respond to inputs

Second-order differential equations describe the dynamic behavior of mechanical, electrical, and control systems when subjected to external forces and oscillations.

Second-order differential equations are widely used to study mechanical, electrical, and control systems. These equations help engineers analyze how a system behaves when subjected to external forces or inputs.

General Form
a(d²y/dx²) + b(dy/dx) + cy = f(x)
a, b, c= constants
y= dependent variable
f(x)= forcing function

The behaviour of such systems changes dramatically depending on whether the forcing function is zero (homogeneous) or non-zero (non-homogeneous). Understanding this distinction is key to predicting and controlling system dynamics.

Solution Technique

Method of Undetermined Coefficients

Finding the particular solution of non-homogeneous differential equations

The method assumes a trial solution that mirrors the form of the forcing function. By substituting into the differential equation, the unknown coefficients are determined to satisfy the equation exactly.

Complete Solution
General Solution = CF + PI
CF= Complementary Function
PI= Particular Integral
  1. Find the complementary function by solving the homogeneous equation
  2. Assume a suitable form for the particular solution based on f(x)
  3. Substitute the assumed solution into the full equation
  4. Determine the unknown constants by comparing coefficients
  5. Obtain the complete general solution
Solved Example
Given Equation
d²y/dx² − 3(dy/dx) + 2y = eˣ
Step 1 — Auxiliary Equation
m² − 3m + 2 = 0
(m−1)(m−2) = 0 → m = 1, 2
Step 2 — Complementary Function
CF = C₁eˣ + C₂e²ˣ
Step 3 — Particular Integral
Assume PI = Axeˣ
Solving gives A = −1
PI = −xeˣ
Complete Solution
y = C₁eˣ + C₂e²ˣ − xeˣ
System Behaviour

Steady-State Response

The long-term behaviour after transient effects have decayed to zero

Phase 01 · Early

Transient Response

  • Exists for a short duration after input
  • Gradually decays with time
  • Depends on initial conditions
  • Dominated by natural frequencies
Phase 02 · Long-term

Steady-State Response

  • Remains after transient response vanishes
  • Depends on the external input
  • Determines long-term system behaviour
  • Matches frequency of forcing function

Total Response = Transient Response + Steady-State Response

Frequency Analysis

Frequency Response

How a system reacts to inputs at different oscillation frequencies

Sinusoidal Input
F(t) = F₀ sin(ωt)
ω= input frequency
F₀= force amplitude
ωₙ= natural frequency

Frequency response describes how a system reacts to different input frequencies. Engineers use it to determine whether a system will amplify, attenuate, or resonate with a given input.

The response depends critically on the relationship between the input frequency ω and the natural frequency ωₙ of the system.

  • Determine system stability margins
  • Identify resonance conditions
  • Evaluate vibration control performance
  • Design filters and damping mechanisms

Critical Phenomenon

Resonance — When ω = ωₙ

The critical condition where the forcing frequency matches the natural frequency

Resonance Condition

Maximum Amplitude

ω = ωₙ = √(k/m)

When the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency of the system, energy is continuously transferred into the system's oscillation, causing amplitude to grow — potentially without bound in undamped systems.

Natural Frequency Formula
ωₙ = √(k/m)
Effects of Resonance
  • Large amplitude vibrations
  • Excessive mechanical noise
  • Structural fatigue in components
  • Potential catastrophic failure
  • Damage to surrounding structures

Real-World Impact

Examples of Resonance

Where resonance appears — and why engineers must account for it

🌉
Bridge Vibrations
Wind can excite a bridge at its natural frequency, causing destructive oscillations (Tacoma Narrows, 1940).
🫧
Washing Machines
Excessive vibrations during the spin cycle when unbalanced loads excite the machine's natural frequency.
🏢
Buildings & Earthquakes
Seismic waves at a building's natural frequency can produce resonance, amplifying structural damage significantly.
⚙
Mechanical Systems
Rotating shafts, turbines, and engines can fail catastrophically if operated at or near their critical resonance speed.
Frequency Behaviour

Response Interpretation

Low Frequency Region · r < 1

Small Response

System follows the input closely. Amplitude ratio near unity. The quasi-static regime — like slowly pushing a spring.

At Natural Frequency · r = 1

Maximum Response

System amplifies the input dramatically. Peak amplitude occurs here — the resonance condition. Dangerous without sufficient damping.

High Frequency Region · r > 1

Reduced Response

System cannot follow the rapidly changing input. Amplitude decreases. At very high frequencies the system effectively filters out the input.
