Second Order
Differential Equation

Introduction

The General Form

Equations containing the second derivative — modelling acceleration, vibration, oscillation

General Form
a(d²y/dx²) + b(dy/dx) + cy = f(x)
a, b, c= constants
y= dependent variable
f(x)= forcing function

Unlike first-order equations, second-order equations can model oscillatory behaviour and energy storage — making them essential across engineering and physics.

Interpretation

The displacement decreases with time. The system returns to equilibrium without oscillating — the hallmark of a critically damped system.

This represents the fastest return to equilibrium without any overshoot — ideal for precision instruments and automatic doors.

Classification

Types of Damping

Three distinct responses depending on the damping ratio ζ = c / (2√mk)

Underdamped

Oscillates to equilibrium

c² < 4mk  |  ζ < 1
  • Oscillates about equilibrium
  • Amplitude decreases gradually
  • Most common practical system
  • Complex conjugate roots
Examples: vehicle suspension, building vibrations, robotic joints
Critically Damped

Fastest return, no overshoot

c² = 4mk  |  ζ = 1
  • Fastest return to equilibrium
  • No oscillations whatsoever
  • Most efficient damping
  • Repeated real roots
Examples: automatic doors, precision measuring devices
Overdamped

Slow, sluggish return

c² > 4mk  |  ζ > 1
  • No oscillations
  • Very slow return to rest
  • Excessive energy dissipation
  • Two distinct real roots
Examples: heavy industrial shock absorbers, hydraulic door closers

Conclusion

Second-order differential equations model the interplay between inertia, damping, and restoring forces — the foundation of all vibration analysis.

Understanding the three damping cases is critical: underdamped systems are common in practice, critically damped systems are optimal for control applications, and overdamped systems appear in heavy industrial equipment.
